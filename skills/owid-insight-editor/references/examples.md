# Example Data Insights by Hannah Ritchie

Three published OWID Data Insights, one per topic the user rotates through. They show the target format and voice. Excerpts are verbatim, © Our World in Data, CC BY 4.0. Fetched 2026-11-10 from the OWID Data Insights Atom feed (`https://ourworldindata.org/atom-data-insights.xml`).

**Limits:** the 100-character title and 850-character text limits come from the Writer application form (<https://ourworldindata.org/hiring-writer-2026>, item 3). Published insights are not held to them: two of the three below exceed one. Hold the user's draft to the form's limits whatever these examples do.

The shared shape: chart title = insight title (a claim), chart subtitle = the metric with its unit and scope, one chart annotation carrying the headline number, footer `Data source: <Producer>, <Dataset> (<year>)`. Short paragraphs, plain words, first person ("I’ve shown").

## 1. Health: HIV/AIDS

<https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/at-the-peak-of-the-hiv-epidemic-aids-caused-more-than-half-of-all-deaths-in-some-countries>, 2026-10-20

- **Title** (91 chars): "At the peak of the HIV epidemic, AIDS caused more than half of all deaths in some countries"
- **Text:** 776 chars, within the limit.
- **Stake:** "More than 800,000 people still die from HIV/AIDS every year."
- **The number:** "At the height of the epidemic, more than half of all deaths in Zimbabwe and Botswana, and over 40% in South Africa and Namibia, were caused by the disease."
- **Caveat / today:** "But about one in five deaths in these countries was still caused by AIDS in 2023."
- **Chart:** line chart, 1980–2023. Title repeats the insight title. Subtitle: "Share of all deaths caused by HIV/AIDS in four hard-hit countries." Annotation: "In 2001, AIDS caused nearly 60% of deaths in Botswana and Zimbabwe". A second note explains the 2021 dip (COVID-19 deaths). Footer: "Data source: IHME, Global Burden of Disease (2025)".
- **How the chart backs the claim:** it plots share of all deaths, not counts, so countries of different sizes compare fairly; the peak the title names is the highest point on the chart and is labelled with the number.

## 2. Poverty: China and Indonesia

<https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/china-reduced-extreme-poverty-fast-but-indonesia-hasnt-been-far-behind>, 2026-10-13

- **Title** (72 chars): "China reduced extreme poverty fast, but Indonesia hasn’t been far behind"
- **Text:** 922 chars, 72 over the form's limit.
- **Popular claim tested:** "Some might assume that China is the only reason why global extreme poverty has declined. But that’s wrong: many countries have seen dramatic reductions in poverty, too."
- **The number:** "In 1984, 86% of Indonesians lived on less than $3 per day. By 2025, this had fallen to less than 4%."
- **Caveat:** "Note that the international poverty line is extremely low, defined as people living on less than $3 per day."
- **Chart:** line chart, 1984–2025. Title repeats the insight title, with the two country names coloured as the lines. Subtitle: "Share of people living below the International Poverty Line of $3 a day, adjusted for inflation and differences in living costs between countries." Annotations: "In 1984, 91% of people in China and 86% in Indonesia lived in extreme poverty" and "3.7% of Indonesians were in extreme poverty in 2025". Footer: "Data source: World Bank Poverty and Inequality Platform (2026)".
- **How the chart backs the claim:** both countries share one metric, one poverty line and one start year, so the "not far behind" comparison is fair; the subtitle carries the inflation and living-cost adjustment so the text does not have to.

## 3. Demographics: age structure

<https://ourworldindata.org/data-insights/one-in-ten-people-in-the-world-is-over-65-how-does-this-compare-in-the-youngest-and-oldest-countries>, 2026-10-26

- **Title** (103 chars): "One in ten people in the world is over 65 — how does this compare in the youngest and oldest countries?" Over the form's limit, and half question.
- **Text:** 828 chars, within the limit.
- **Stake / the number:** "One in four people in the world is younger than 15. Just one in ten is 65 or older."
- **Contrast:** "In the Central African Republic, half of the population is under 15. Being over 65 is pretty rare: just 2% of the population." / "Japan is the opposite. … Almost one-third of the population is 65 and older"
- **Why it matters:** "These differences in age distribution matter a lot for a country’s infrastructure, socioeconomic conditions, and public spending — from pensions and healthcare to childcare and education."
- **Chart:** three stacked 100% bars (Central African Republic, global average, Japan). Title: "Age structures vary hugely, from the youngest countries to the oldest", which differs from the insight title. Subtitle: "Share of the population that was under 15, 15 to 64 years old, and 65 and over in 2025." Footer: "Data source: UN, World Population Prospects (2024). Projections for 2025."
- **How the chart backs the claim:** every bar uses the same three age bands and the same year, and the global average sits between the two extremes as the reference point; each number in the text is printed on a bar.
