# Essentialism: The Disciplined Pursuit of Less (Greg McKeown, 2014)

## Core argument

Almost everything is noise; a vital few efforts yield exponentially greater results. Trying to do everything forces trade-offs by default, producing a millimeter of progress in a million directions. Essentialism is a system to explore broadly, discern the vital few, eliminate the trivial many, and build buffers and routines for effortless execution.

## Practices

### 1. Schedule distraction-free space to think and read

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 5 "ESCAPE: The Perks of Being Unavailable", p. 59–67
- **Do:** Block phone- and e-mail-free time to think and read. The book's examples: one executive keeps up to 2 hours a day blank, in 30-minute blocks; one company holds a full-day gathering without phones or e-mail on the first Monday of each month; the author wrote from 5:00 a.m. to 1:00 p.m. five days a week while writing the book. He also reads classic literature for the first 20 minutes of his day, before e-mail.
- **Why:** Constant responsiveness prevents stepping back to separate the vital few from noise.

### 2. Keep a minimal daily journal with a 90-day review

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 6 "LOOK: See What Really Matters", p. 73
- **Do:** Write less than you feel like writing daily until journaling sticks. Every 90 days, spend 1 hour reading that quarter's entries to spot broader trends rather than minor details.
- **Why:** Overwriting causes procrastination; quarterly reviews reveal cumulative patterns missed daily.

### 3. Protect non-instrumental play

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 7 "PLAY: Embrace the Wisdom of Your Inner Child", p. 77–83
- **Do:** Mine childhood memories for activities done purely for joy and schedule similar play—for its own sake, not as a means to an end.
- **Why:** Play broadens options, calms amygdala stress responses, and stimulates executive functions.

### 4. Protect sleep and allow naps

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 8 "SLEEP: Protect the Asset", p. 84–94
- **Do:** Treat sleep as a priority and refuse late tasks in order to get it; the author aims for 8 hours a night. Consider naps (his example: a 30-minute session in a nap pod). In the study the book cites, top violinists averaged 8.6 hours of sleep per 24 hours plus 2.8 hours of afternoon naps a week.
- **Why:** Four to five hours a night for a week impairs cognition like a 0.1% blood alcohol level. In one puzzle study, twice as many participants found the solution after 8 uninterrupted hours of sleep as in the sleep-deprived group.

### 5. Filter choices with the 90 Percent Rule and extreme criteria

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 9 "SELECT: The Power of Extreme Criteria", p. 95–106
- **Do:** Score an option 0–100 on its top criterion; below 90 percent, reset to 0 and reject. For opportunities, list 3 minimum and 3 extreme criteria; reject unless it passes all 3 minimum and at least 2 extreme criteria. Test career choices against passion, talent, and world need.
- **Why:** Broad criteria trap you in merely good commitments that crowd out the top 10 percent.
- **Limits:** Requires rejecting good options and waiting for a clear yes.

### 6. Set one essential intent and use the "no" repertoire

- **Cite:** Part 3, Ch. 10 "CLARIFY: One Decision That Makes a Thousand", p. 110–120; Ch. 11 "DARE: The Power of a Graceful 'No'", p. 121–131
- **Do:** Write one concrete, measurable essential intent (what to excel at and how you know you are done). Refuse misaligned requests via eight tactics: count to 3; soft "no but"; "Let me check my calendar"; email bounceback; "Yes, what should I deprioritize?"; humor; "You are welcome to X, I am willing to Y"; or referring others.
- **Why:** Clear intent settles a thousand decisions, and polite refusals trade popularity for respect.

### 7. Uncommit via zero-based budgeting and reverse pilots

- **Cite:** Part 3, Ch. 12 "UNCOMMIT: Win Big by Cutting Your Losses", p. 132–140
- **Do:** Ask what you would sacrifice to join a project today if not already in it. Use zero-based budgeting: wipe all commitments on paper and justify each from scratch. Pause 5 seconds before speaking, and run a reverse pilot by stopping a task for days or weeks to test if anyone notices.
- **Why:** Sunk-cost bias, the endowment effect, and fear of waste keep dead commitments alive.

### 8. Set advance boundaries, dealbreakers, and social contracts

- **Cite:** Part 3, Ch. 14 "LIMIT: The Freedom of Setting Boundaries", p. 148–153
- **Do:** Set limits before requests arrive. Help others without taking over problems they should solve themselves. Log pinches of resentment to list dealbreakers, and agree on upfront social contracts stating your priorities and limits.
- **Why:** Advance limits eliminate repeated refusals and protect personal time from erosion.
- **Limits:** Hard limits can carry career costs if a manager or team resists.

### 9. Add 50 percent time buffers and run scenario planning

- **Cite:** Part 4, Ch. 15 "BUFFER: The Unfair Advantage", p. 156–163
- **Do:** Add 50 percent to time estimates (block 30 extra minutes for a 60-minute call; leave 15 minutes early for a 10-minute drive) and prepare early. Answer five risk questions for major projects: risks, worst case, social effects, financial impact, and resilience investments (e.g., 20 percent budget buffer).
- **Why:** In one study of 37 students, only 30 percent finished a thesis within their own estimate; buffers absorb that error and prevent last-minute rushing.

### 10. Rewire habit cues, start small, and do the hardest thing first

- **Cite:** Part 4, Ch. 17 "PROGRESS: The Power of Small Wins", p. 170–177; Ch. 18 "FLOW: The Genius of Routine", p. 178–187
- **Do:** Start early with minimal viable preparation: weeks ahead of a presentation, spend 4 minutes noting ideas for it (a colleague's version: 15 seconds writing a meeting's objectives when scheduling it). Track wins visually. Swap an essential routine onto an existing cue, pair new cues with existing habits, do the hardest task first, theme weekdays, and change one routine at a time.
- **Why:** About 40 percent of choices are unconscious habits, and small wins sustain momentum.

### 11. Remove the slowest hiker

- **Cite:** Part 4, Ch. 16 "SUBTRACT: Bring Forth More by Removing Obstacles", p. 167–169
- **Do:** (1) State the outcome precisely enough to know when it is done. (2) Take a few minutes to list every obstacle between you and that outcome, then pick the one whose removal would make most of the others disappear: the "slowest hiker". (3) Remove that one before touching the next. Apparently productive work, such as more research or polishing a first draft, can itself be the obstacle.
- **Why:** Only one obstacle is the constraint at any time; removing others changes nothing while it stays.

### 12. Ask what is important now

- **Cite:** Part 4, Ch. 19 "FOCUS: What’s Important Now?", p. 194–195
- **Do:** When many tasks compete for attention, stop. List everything pulling at you and cross off what is not important right now. On a separate page, list what you might want to do someday; that list is a record of ideas, not of commitments. Then work through the first list one item at a time.
- **Why:** Writing the future possibilities down keeps the ideas and removes the pressure to act on them at once.

## Positions

- **Saying no vs. exploring:** Explore broadly first, then reject everything below the top 10 percent (Part 2, Ch. 9).
- **Quitting vs. finishing:** Uncommit whenever a commitment fails zero-based justification, rejecting sunk-cost bias (Part 3, Ch. 12).
- **Scheduling:** Protect blank thinking space daily and add 50 percent time buffers instead of best-case schedules (Part 2, Ch. 5; Part 4, Ch. 15).
- **Ambition and goals:** Reject plural priorities; pursue one essential intent via minimal viable wins (Ch. 1; Part 3, Ch. 10; Part 4, Ch. 17).
- **Passion:** Pair deep passion with talent and a significant world need (Part 2, Ch. 9).
- **Rest and limits:** Protect sleep, play, and personal boundaries instead of giving them up to constant work; the author aims for 8 hours of sleep a night (Part 2, Ch. 7–8; Part 3, Ch. 14).
- **Choosing projects:** Apply extreme criteria (3 minimum, 3 extreme, 90 Percent Rule) rather than straddling strategies (Part 1, Ch. 4; Part 2, Ch. 9).

## Terms

- **Paradox of success:** Clarity brings success, which brings options, which diffuses effort (Ch. 1).
- **Straddled strategy:** Keeping an existing path while adding an incompatible new one (Part 1, Ch. 4).
- **90 Percent Rule:** Scoring 0–100 on one criterion and turning any score under 90 into zero (Part 2, Ch. 9).
- **Essential intent:** One concrete, measurable decision settling a thousand future choices (Part 3, Ch. 10).
- **Reverse pilot:** Stopping a task for days or weeks to test if anyone notices (Part 3, Ch. 12).
- **Minimal viable preparation:** Starting weeks ahead with a tiny step like 4 minutes (Part 4, Ch. 17).
- **Kairos vs. chronos:** Qualitative presence (*kairos*) versus clock time (*chronos*) (Part 4, Ch. 19).
