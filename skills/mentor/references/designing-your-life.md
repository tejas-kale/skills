# Designing Your Life (Bill Burnett and Dave Evans, 2016)

## Core argument

No single passion or ideal life awaits discovery; most people can live multiple fulfilling lives. Instead of analyzing your way to one answer, apply design thinking—curiosity, bias to action, reframing, awareness, radical collaboration—via small prototypes. Assess where you stand, build a compass, track engagement and energy, draft parallel five-year plans, and test options via conversations and trials. Happiness comes from narrowing choices, choosing discerningly, letting go, and learning from failure.

## Practices

### 1. Accept non-actionable (gravity) problems

- **Cite:** Ch. 1 "Start Where You Are", p. 31–35; Ch. 4 "Getting Unstuck", p. 88–89
- **Do:** Check if a stuck problem is actionable. If it is a fact of nature or market reality unlikely to change (a gravity problem), accept it and redirect effort to an actionable workaround or new problem. If stalled on one failed solution (an anchor problem), reframe it and test small alternatives.
- **Why:** Non-actionable problems trap you permanently; acceptance frees attention for solvable work.
- **Limits:** Do not mistake a hard, actionable problem for a gravity problem.

### 2. Health / Work / Play / Love dashboard

- **Cite:** Ch. 1 "Start Where You Are", p. 35–46
- **Do:** Write a few sentences on health (body, mind, spirit), work (paid and unpaid), play (joy for its own sake), and love. Mark each gauge from 0 to Full, spot red lights, and check for gravity problems.
- **Why:** You cannot choose a direction until you know your starting point and imbalances.
- **Limits:** Gauges need not be equal; aim for a workable mix.

### 3. Workview and Lifeview compass

- **Cite:** Ch. 2 "Building a Compass", p. 48–56; Conclusion "A Well-Designed Life", p. 200–201
- **Do:** Spend ~30 minutes writing a ~250-word Workview (why you work) and up to 30 minutes writing a ~250-word Lifeview (meaning, values). Answer three integration questions: where they complement, clash, and drive each other. Recalibrate annually.
- **Why:** Aligning who you are, what you believe, and what you do prevents living by another's compass.

### 4. Good Time Journal and AEIOU reflection

- **Cite:** Ch. 3 "Wayfinding", p. 59–73
- **Do:** Log activities daily (or at least twice weekly) for three weeks, rating engagement (noting flow) and energy. Reflect weekly using AEIOU: Activities, Environments, Interactions, Objects, Users. Between roles, log past peak experiences.
- **Why:** Engagement and energy clues reveal what sustains you so you can tune your role or steer next steps.
- **Limits:** Avoid revisionist memory on past experiences; stop reflecting once patterns are clear.

### 5. Three-map mind mapping

- **Cite:** Ch. 4 "Getting Unstuck", p. 80–83, 89–93
- **Do:** Pick one engagement, one energy, and one flow item from your journal. Spend 3–5 minutes per map writing 5–6 first-ring words, branching 3–4 layers to 12+ outer-ring items without censoring. Combine three disparate outer-ring words per map into a job description, name it, and draw a napkin sketch.
- **Why:** Fast visual association bypasses your inner critic and prevents clinging to your first idea.

### 6. Three five-year Odyssey Plans

- **Cite:** Ch. 5 "Design Your Lives", p. 96–110
- **Do:** Draft three distinct five-year plans: Life One (current path), Life Two (if Life One vanished), Life Three (if money and image were no object). Add a timeline, six-word title, 2–3 questions, and four gauges (Resources, Likability, Confidence, Coherence). Share with 2–5 supporters who reflect without critiquing.
- **Why:** Parallel plans prevent premature commitment and reveal multiple viable futures.

### 7. Prototyping conversations (Life Design Interviews)

- **Cite:** Ch. 6 "Prototyping", p. 117–118; Ch. 8 "Designing Your Dream Job", p. 140–142
- **Do:** Request a 30-minute coffee (via referral if possible) with someone doing work you want to explore to hear their story and daily reality; never call it an interview or ask for a job. Later, ask what steps would explore how someone like you might join.
- **Why:** Real stories test assumptions cheaply and open the hidden job market.
- **Limits:** Fails if perceived as a disguised job interview.

### 8. Prototype experiences and group brainstorming

- **Cite:** Ch. 6 "Prototyping", p. 119–126
- **Do:** Test an Odyssey Plan question via a small real-world trial (one-day shadowing, one-week project, or short internship). When stuck, run a 20–60 minute brainstorm with 3–6 people: frame a "How many ways..." question, warm up, generate ideas for quantity without judgment, then group, name, and silently vote with colored dots.
- **Why:** Hands-on trials produce real data and let you fail fast at low cost.

### 9. Four-step choosing process and grokking

- **Cite:** Ch. 9 "Choosing Happiness", p. 150–165
- **Do:** (1) Gather options; (2) cross off extras until at most five remain; (3) choose discerningly using analysis plus gut knowing—optionally grokking each top option for 1–3 days as if chosen, with at least a day or two of reset after each; (4) let go of unchosen options and invest in the chosen path.
- **Why:** Most people choose well among three to five options and worse beyond that; keeping unchosen options open lowers satisfaction with the choice made.
- **Limits:** Keep grokking private so others are not misled.

### 10. Failure reframe log

- **Cite:** Ch. 10 "Failure Immunity", p. 174–180
- **Do:** Once or twice monthly, log failures and sort them into screwups (move on), weaknesses (recurring traits—avoid triggers), or growth opportunities (preventable with a fix). Note the critical failure factor and next-time change.
- **Why:** Sorting preventable lessons from fixed traits builds failure immunity.

### 11. Build a Life Design Team and seek counsel

- **Cite:** Ch. 11 "Building a Team", p. 183–193
- **Do:** Form a 3–5 person team (3–6 including you) meeting regularly under four rules: respectful, confidential, participative, generative. Inform close family early. Seek mentors for counsel (questions clarifying your thinking) over advice ("If I were you").
- **Why:** Life design is co-created; pairs lack diversity, and teams over six force rigid roles.

## Positions

- **Narrowing vs. exploring:** Explore broadly first—three mind maps, three Odyssey Plans, many prototypes (Ch. 4–5); then cut lists to at most five options to avoid paralysis (Ch. 9).
- **Quitting vs. finishing:** Never cling to a first idea or failed solution; fail fast on prototypes and pivot, because life is an infinite game with endless correction shots (Ch. 1, Ch. 4, Ch. 10).
- **Scheduling and planning:** Reject rigid plans; plan in five-year Odyssey horizons (two-to-four-year seasons), wayfind step by step, and schedule around Good Time Journal energy flows (Intro, Ch. 3, Ch. 5).
- **Ambition and goals:** Tests ambitious changes with small prototypes before a leap, and judges success against the person's own Workview and Lifeview. The method does not say which ambitions or way of life to choose; large goals, money and career may be central if they fit (Ch. 1, Ch. 2, Ch. 5, Conclusion).
- **Passion as a guide:** Rejects needing a known passion before starting: the authors estimate that 80 percent of people do not know what they are passionate about, and that passion usually comes after trying something and getting good at it (Introduction, Ch. 6).
- **Rest and limits on work:** Daily "perfect pie" balance is a myth; balance happens over time (Conclusion). Yet play and health are core gauges, and draining tasks require rest (Ch. 1, Ch. 3).
- **Choosing skills or projects:** Treats career-test results as often inconclusive and hard to act on (Introduction). Explores work through prototype conversations and experiences, rates plans on Resources, Likability, Confidence and Coherence, and combines analysis with gut discernment (Ch. 5, Ch. 6, Ch. 9).

## Terms

- **Dysfunctional belief**: False myth blocking life design, paired with a reframe (Intro).
- **Wicked problem**: Open-ended problem with no single right solution (Ch. 1).
- **Gravity problem**: Non-actionable circumstance that must be accepted (Ch. 1).
- **Coherency**: Alignment of who you are, what you believe, and what you do (Ch. 2).
- **Wayfinding**: Navigating without a map via engagement and energy clues (Ch. 3).
- **Anchor problem**: Doable problem stalled by clinging to one unworkable solution (Ch. 4).
- **Odyssey Plan**: One of three five-year plans with timeline, title, questions, and gauges (Ch. 5).
- **Life Design Interview**: 30-minute prototype conversation to hear a story, not seek a job (Ch. 6).
- **Hidden job market**: ~80 percent of jobs never posted online, reached via referrals (Ch. 8).
- **Grokking**: Living mentally inside one choice for 1–3 days to test fit (Ch. 9).
- **Failure immunity**: Treating setbacks as learning data in an infinite game (Ch. 10).
