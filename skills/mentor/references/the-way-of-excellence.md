# The Way of Excellence (Brad Stulberg, 2026)

## Core argument

Excellence is involved engagement in pursuits supporting your values and goals, combining mastery and mattering. Humans are wired for it, and pursuing it counters alienation, distraction, and zombie burnout. Unlike perfectionism or obsession, excellence pairs deliberate challenge with genuine rest, sustained through fit, deep focus, consistency, discipline, patience, community, and joy.

## Practices

### 1. Align goals to values and process steps

- **Cite:** Part 1, Ch. 2 "The Psychology of Excellence", p. 35; Part 2, Ch. 5 "Goals", p. 71–77
- **Do:** Review your core values at least yearly (the book says most people have three to five), pick a goal just outside your comfort zone, and break it into controllable steps. Judge yourself by presence and effort; when fixating on outcomes, ask what single step helps now—or rest.
- **Why:** Growth happens during the pursuit. Fixating on the outcome leads to reckless pursuit (summit fever), and expecting the achievement itself to bring lasting happiness is the arrival fallacy.
- **Limits:** SMART goals are most useful for beginners; as expertise grows, broader goals may help when specific targets start to constrain.

### 2. Set minimum effective doses for other life rooms

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 7 "Trade-Offs", p. 93–95
- **Do:** Drop equal balance when going all in, and set minimum effective doses for other identity-house rooms (e.g., 30 minutes daily exercise, family 6:30–8:30 p.m. four evenings weekly, friends twice monthly). Use self-distancing to audit trade-offs.
- **Why:** Minimum doses protect health and relationships without guilt during your main work.

### 3. Strip work to the 99.9 percent fundamentals

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 7 "Trade-Offs", p. 97–99
- **Do:** List your craft's unchanging fundamentals alongside tempting tools and fads. Prioritize fundamentals first and cut complexity.
- **Why:** Complexity gives procrastination room; simplicity directs consistent effort to the fundamentals that matter most. The 99.9 figure is the book's illustration, not a measurement.

### 4. Run scheduled deep-focus blocks device-free

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 8 "Focus", p. 104–108
- **Do:** Schedule deep-focus blocks of 50 minutes to 2 hours, as many as the pursuit and other responsibilities allow (the book's examples: three a week for one person, three a day for another), and remove phones from the room. Accept initial restlessness, park stray thoughts in a paper notebook, disable distracting apps for two weeks (or use an offline second device), and take 5-to-30-minute breaks afterward.
- **Why:** Even a visible silent phone degrades performance, and task-switching erodes attention and mood.
- **Limits:** Under 50 minutes prevents settling in; past 2 hours brings fatigue. Rebuilding focus takes weeks.

### 5. Raise the floor on bad days

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 6 "Consistency", p. 80–87
- **Do:** Keep daily workload near your four-week average and stop slightly short (one rep or 30 minutes early) to protect tomorrow. On off days, lower the target (e.g., 200–300 words instead of 1,000) to avoid zeros, and apply a next-play mentality after errors.
- **Why:** Regular small deposits compound and raise your average, while heroic spikes risk burnout or injury.

### 6. Start with behavioral activation, not mood

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 9 "Discipline", p. 112–117
- **Do:** Never wait for motivation or prejudge performance when feeling flat. Start your planned work at a fixed time, bring low mood along, and pair firm self-discipline with self-kindness.
- **Why:** Action creates motivation, whereas fighting unwanted thoughts and emotions strengthens them.
- **Limits:** Excess constraints breed rigidity; you can still stop after starting if genuinely depleted.

### 7. Practice genuine rest and a weekly digital sabbath

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 10 "Renewal", p. 122–128
- **Do:** Choose rest requiring no self-control, free of work or anxiety triggers (no scrolling, email, or news between gym sets or work blocks), without letting measurement turn recovery into work. Prioritize 7–9 hours of sleep (plus 10–30 minute naps), 6+ minute walks, friends, nature (or 6 minutes viewing greenspace), and a weekly digital sabbath (e.g., Saturday to Sunday morning).
- **Why:** Stress plus rest equals growth; genuine rest calms the nervous system and fuels insight, while stressful screen use and obsessive recovery tracking obstruct it.
- **Limits:** Ditch sleep or recovery trackers if they cause stress or turn rest into work.

### 8. Decondition distraction in everyday waiting

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 12 "Patience", p. 141
- **Do:** Leave your phone in the car during errands so you practice waiting in lines unstimulated. When a dinner companion steps away, sit without checking your phone.
- **Why:** Enduring brief unstimulated gaps weakens your craving for novelty and speed, training patience for long plateaus.

### 9. Align work with your chronotype and mark transitions

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 13 "Routine", p. 152–156
- **Do:** Find your lark or owl chronotype (ideally via a 7-day no-alarm, no-stimulant reset) and schedule hard work at peak alertness, saving email and meetings for low-energy hours. Set three daily, three weekly, and three monthly practices, plus small transition rituals (e.g., 15 minutes reading to start, a short walk to end).
- **Why:** Matching work to energy and marking transitions prevents days from blurring together.
- **Limits:** Keep routines rugged and flexible; release when life demands it without making exceptions chronic.

### 10. Break ruts with the 48-hour rule and stepping away

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 14 "Gumption", p. 159–164
- **Do:** Set a time limit for celebrating a win or grieving a defeat, then return to the work. Start from 48 hours and adjust to the size of the event: an afternoon for a small one, a week or two for a large one. When stuck, stack a small high-percentage win, step away at circular edits or boredom, explore outside your domain, and keep an ego-check partner.
- **Why:** Returning to the work steadies emotion, stepping away unlocks insight, and outside ideas plus honest peers prevent hubris.

### 11. Reframe pre-effort nerves and run safe-to-fail experiments

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 15 "Curiosity", p. 167–173
- **Do:** Before hard effort, use the mantra "brave new world" and relabel arousal as excitement rather than threat. Carry an analog notebook as an ethnographer, and when plateaued, identify variables you could adjust and run safe-to-fail experiments, where a failure does no serious harm.
- **Why:** Curiosity inhibits brain fear pathways, and small experiments sustain engagement once beginner gains slow.

### 12. Quit, fit, grit

- **Cite:** Part 2, Ch. 4 "Care", p. 61–64
- **Do:** Try a range of pursuits and give each some time. Quit freely until one shows fit: it matches your values and natural abilities, you stay curious about it, and you see room to grow. Once fit is there, switch to persistence and stay through the ups and downs. Signs of fit: a pull to keep showing up, a general sense of progress, and growing satisfaction, even when single days are bad.
- **Why:** Persisting before fit keeps a person in the wrong pursuit too long; quitting after fit, in search of something better, means never staying long enough to excel.

## Positions

- **Saying no and narrowing versus exploring:** Sample widely to find fit (values, ability, curiosity, growth), then narrow in and exploit one path (Ch. 4).
- **Quitting versus finishing:** Rejects "never quit" dogma; quit early without fit, and apply grit only after fit is found (Ch. 4).
- **Scheduling and planning:** Backs anchor routines, scheduled deep-focus blocks, and chronotype alignment, while rejecting rigid optimization (Ch. 8, Ch. 13).
- **Ambition, goals, and workload:** Rejects balance and backs going all in seasonally, while warning against chronic obsession, summit fever, and neglecting minimum doses elsewhere (Ch. 5, Ch. 7).
- **Passion as a guide:** Rejects lightning-bolt passion; follow curiosity and values, build passion over years, and guard harmonious passion against obsessive passion (Ch. 4, Ch. 18).
- **Rest, leisure, and limits:** Rest is necessary for sustained growth. Avoid rest that triggers stress or becomes work, such as scrolling or news; keep a recovery tracker only if it helps you rest (Ch. 10).
- **Choosing skills or projects:** Pick pursuits aligned with 3–5 core values offering autonomy, competence, and belonging, at your skill edge, focusing on 99.9 percent fundamentals (Ch. 2, Ch. 5, Ch. 7).

## Terms

- **Homeostatic upregulation:** Biological drive toward flourishing using feelings as guides (Ch. 1).
- **Zombie burnout:** Exhaustion from too little meaningful work rather than overwork (Ch. 2).
- **Shitty flow:** Effortless absorption in low-value scrolling that leaves shame afterward (Ch. 2).
- **Internet brain:** Hypervigilant, attention-fractured state from excessive online time (Ch. 2).
- **Identity house:** Multi-roomed self-complexity that stabilizes you during setbacks (Ch. 4).
- **Raising the floor:** Lowering targets on bad days to avoid zeros (Ch. 6).
- **Minimum effective dose:** Minimum time needed to maintain secondary identity rooms (Ch. 7).
- **Algorithmic mass distraction:** Engineered stream of attention-capturing interruptions (Ch. 8).
- **Gumption traps:** Internal or external obstacles that drain enthusiasm (Ch. 14).
- **Forty-eight-hour rule:** A flexible time limit, nominally 48 hours, on dwelling on a result before returning to the work (Ch. 14).
